Buzzend Growth & Product Strategy 2026

Purpose: A practical, execution-focused strategy for making Buzzend successful.
This document replaces the original long-form marketing strategy with a simpler operating plan while preserving the important product, market, positioning, content, analytics, growth and experimentation work from the original document.

1. Executive Decision

The decision in one paragraph

Do not reposition Buzzend as a kids or school fitness app right now.

The strongest current opportunity is still:

Buzzend is a camera-powered workout companion that counts reps automatically and provides real-time coaching while people exercise.

The first target should remain beginner-to-intermediate home exercisers, approximately 18--35, especially people who want simple bodyweight workouts, accountability, visible progress and less manual counting.

The client request about children and schools is valuable, but it should be treated as a market-expansion hypothesis, not a reason to immediately change the product.

The recommended order is:

PHASE 1
Adult Consumer Buzzend
        ↓
Prove activation + retention + referral
        ↓
PHASE 2
Parent-Guided Family Experiment
        ↓
Validate parent demand + trust + willingness to pay
        ↓
PHASE 3
Family MVP if validation is strong
        ↓
PHASE 4
Teen / School discovery
        ↓
PHASE 5
School / B2B product only if schools show real demand

2. Why We Are Not Pivoting to Children Yet

The current Buzzend product is built around camera-based movement recognition, rep counting and coaching. That is a strong consumer proposition, but making children a target audience changes much more than marketing.

A child-focused direction can affect:

  • App Store and Google Play declarations
  • Privacy architecture
  • Data collection
  • Camera handling
  • Analytics SDKs
  • Advertising
  • Age handling
  • Parental controls
  • Consent flows
  • Social features
  • Messaging
  • Sharing
  • Account architecture
  • Data retention
  • Customer support
  • Legal/compliance review
  • School procurement and administration

Apple's current guidance places additional requirements on Kids Category apps, including parental gates and stronger restrictions around third-party analytics, advertising and transmission of children's personal/device information. Apple also distinguishes a parental gate from legal parental consent for personal-data collection. Apple App Review Guidelines and Apple Kids guidance.

Google Play's Families policies require additional protections when children are part of the target audience. Google specifically identifies camera sensor data as sensitive information from children and imposes restrictions on identifiers, SDKs and data practices. Google Play Families Policy.

For services directed to children under 13 in the United States, COPPA can require parental notice and verifiable parental consent before collecting personal information. The FTC specifically treats photographs, videos and audio containing children's images/voices as personal information in relevant contexts. FTC COPPA guidance.

Therefore:

A parent-guided product may be a good future opportunity, but it should not be added casually to the existing adult product.

Compliance sources are directional product-planning references, not legal advice. A qualified privacy/legal review is required before launching a child-directed experience.

3. The Current Buzzend Product

3.1 Product summary

Buzzend is a mobile fitness experience centered around:

  • Real-time camera-based exercise recognition
  • Automatic repetition counting
  • Form/coaching cues
  • Voice coaching
  • Workout goals
  • Streaks
  • Challenges
  • Social/progress mechanics

The current supported exercise set is:

  1. Squat
  2. Push-up
  3. Lunge
  4. Sit-up
  5. Jumping Jack

The product's most important characteristic is that the phone camera participates in the workout.

3.2 The core experience

Put phone down
      ↓

      ↓
Camera becomes ready
      ↓
Start moving
      ↓
Buzzend detects movement
      ↓
Rep is counted
      ↓
Coaching/cue is provided
      ↓

      ↓

      ↓

3.3 What should be the hero?

The workout itself.

The strongest "wow" moment is:

The user moves and Buzzend counts/coaches in real time.

Social posting is supporting functionality.

Do not make the social feed the main product story.

4. The Problem Buzzend Should Own

Do not try to own the entire fitness category.

Own this narrower problem:

"I want to work out at home, but I don't want to guess, manually count everything, or work out completely alone."

Buzzend answers three questions:

1. What am I doing?

Coaching and movement guidance.

2. What did I do?

Automatic rep counting.

3. How do I keep going?

Goals, streaks, challenges and progress.

This is a much stronger story than simply saying:

"Buzzend is an AI fitness app."

5. Positioning

Primary positioning

For people who want to work out at home without constantly guessing or manually counting, Buzzend is a camera-powered workout companion that counts reps and provides real-time coaching as you move.

Simple consumer explanation

Buzzend uses your phone camera to count your workout reps and coach you while you exercise.

Short explanation

Put your phone down. Start moving. Buzzend counts and coaches.

Brand promise

Make every rep easier to understand, track and repeat.

6. Unique Value Proposition

Primary message

Your phone counts. Your form gets coached. You just move.

Campaign message

Put your phone down. Start moving.

Functional explanation

AI-powered rep counting and real-time workout coaching.

Use the benefit language in consumer marketing.

Do not lead with technical implementation such as:

  • MediaPipe
  • pose landmarks
  • classification algorithms
  • state machines
  • computer-vision architecture

Those are useful for technical/developer content, not as the main consumer message.

7. What Buzzend Should NOT Claim

Avoid claims that are broad, difficult to prove or potentially misleading.

Do not claim:

  • "Perfect form"
  • "Injury-proof workouts"
  • "Replaces your personal trainer"
  • "Guaranteed results"
  • "Guaranteed weight loss"
  • "Medical-grade form analysis"
  • "Most accurate AI"
  • "World's first"
  • "Best fitness app"
  • "Scientifically proven" unless the exact claim is supported by appropriate evidence

Preferred language:

"Buzzend watches your movement and counts your reps."

"Buzzend gives you workout guidance while you move."

"Buzzend can provide form-related coaching cues."

11. The Kids / Parent / School Opportunity

This is now a formal future opportunity, not a current pivot.

        ↓

        ↓

        ↓

        ↓

13. What a Future Child Mode Should NOT Initially Include

If a Family MVP is eventually built, avoid adding all adult social functionality to children.

Initially avoid:

  • Public profiles
  • Open social feeds
  • Open messaging
  • Random friend requests
  • Public child leaderboards
  • Public child videos
  • Location features
  • Targeted advertising
  • Unnecessary personal information
  • Open user-generated content
  • Adult-oriented content
  • Unrestricted external links

Start with:

Parent control → workout → progress → safe family motivation

Safety and trust should come before virality.

14. School Opportunity

Do not build a school dashboard simply because a client asks whether Buzzend can be used at school.

A school product is a different business.

Potential requirements could include:

  • School accounts
  • Administrator accounts
  • Teacher accounts
  • Student management
  • Classroom management
  • Device management
  • Consent workflows
  • Privacy/data policies
  • Assignments
  • Class challenges
  • Reporting
  • Support
  • Procurement
  • Contracts
  • Data retention
  • School-specific controls

Before development, interview schools.

School discovery questions

  1. What problem would Buzzend solve for the school?
  2. Who would use it?
  3. Who would pay?
  4. What age groups?
  5. Would students use personal phones?
  6. Would the school provide devices?
  7. Are cameras allowed in the intended environment?
  8. What would teachers need?
  9. What data can teachers see?
  10. What data must remain private?
  11. What approval process is required?
  12. Would the school actually deploy it?

No strong demand = no school product.

15. Decision Framework for the Kids/School Opportunity

Use this before changing the product.

Step 1 --- Interview

Talk to:

  • 10--20 parents
  • 5--10 teachers
  • 3--5 school decision makers if possible

Step 2 --- Prototype

Create a clickable Family concept.

Do not build the backend.

Step 3 --- Test

Measure:

  • Parent interest
  • Trust
  • Permission concerns
  • Desired controls
  • Desired progress information
  • Willingness to pay
  • Likelihood of actual use

Step 4 --- Decide

Stop

If parents/schools do not show meaningful interest.

Research further

If interest is high but requirements are unclear.

Build Family MVP

If parents show strong demand and trust requirements are understood.

Build School MVP

Only if schools demonstrate a real operational problem and willingness to adopt/pay.

Why people stop scrolling — and the Buzzend content rules

You don't need a psychology degree. You need to know the 12 levers below, and the rule each one gives us. Every video we make should pull at least 2–3 of these levers in the first 3 seconds.

The levers

1 · Attention is a pattern-break. Feeds are endless sameness; the brain stops for the unexpected. A phone on the floor counting a human's squats out loud is unexpected. → Rule: the counter must be visible and moving within the first 2 seconds of every video. Never open with a logo, a greeting, or "hey guys".

2 · Curiosity — an open question the brain must close. "Can AI count my push-ups?" forces the viewer to wait for the answer. → Rule: question hooks must be answered ON CAMERA within ~3 seconds (the counter ticking up = the answer). Never bait without paying off.

3 · Problem recognition — "that's me." People stop for their own problem said plainly: losing count, restarting every Monday, hating the gym, working out alone. → Rule: name the problem in the viewer's words (we collect exact phrases from comments in the hooks-inbox), not in marketing words.

4 · Relatability beats polish. A real person in a normal living room outperforms a studio ad. Slightly imperfect = trustworthy. → Rule: film in real homes, normal clothes, normal bodies. Never make it look like an ad. Beginner energy is our advantage, not a weakness.

5 · Emotion — surprise and delight, not hype. The honest reaction "wait, it actually counted that?" is our emotional core. → Rule: capture genuine reactions. Never script fake excitement.

6 · Social proof — people trust people. Comments, duets, "my friend challenged me", visible member counts. → Rule: screenshot great comments (with permission) and turn them into content. Reply to everything — replies are public proof someone's home.

7 · Authority without arrogance. We earn trust by demonstrating, not claiming. Showing the counter work, including when it misses a sloppy rep, is more convincing than "most accurate AI". → Rule: show, never claim. A missed rep on a bad-form squat is GOOD content ("why didn't this rep count?").

8 · Transformation — before → after. Fitness runs on visible change: day 1 vs day 7 of a streak, 5 shaky push-ups → 20 clean ones. → Rule: build series content (streak diaries, "30 days of…") so transformation accumulates on the profile.

9 · FOMO — used gently. "245 people joined this challenge" works. Fake urgency ("last chance!") destroys trust for an app that's always available. → Rule: only real numbers, only real challenges. No countdown-timer theatrics.

10 · Novelty + specificity. "AI fitness app" is vague noise. "My phone counted 47 squats" is specific and new. → Rule: always use a real number in the hook when we can. "47 squats" beats "my workout". Specific numbers read as true.

11 · Storytelling — even in 20 seconds. Setup (phone goes down) → tension (will it count?) → payoff (it counts) → resolution (result screen). That's a complete story. → Rule: every video has the four beats. If a cut has no payoff moment, it's not done.

12 · Community — belonging keeps people. People stay for people. Challenges-with-friends is literally our product. → Rule: end challenge content with "Challenge a friend." and treat the comment section as the product demo of our community.

CTA psychology (why we say "Try your first AI workout")

  • A CTA works when it's one small, concrete next step. Two CTAs = zero CTAs. Never combine "follow AND download AND comment".
  • "Try your first AI workout" beats "Download Buzzend" because it sells the experience, is low-commitment ("try", "first"), and matches our definition of success (a completed workout, not a download).
  • Say it once, at the end, spoken + on-screen + in the caption. On challenge content, the CTA is "Challenge a friend." instead.

The 6-point pre-publish sanity check

Before anything goes live, it must pass all six:

  1. Counter (or the product truth) visible in the first 2 seconds?
  2. At least 2 levers from this page working in the first 3 seconds?
  3. Would a stranger, muted, understand "the phone counts your reps"?
  4. Is everything shown real (§40 — no faked counts)?
  5. Exactly one CTA?
  6. No banned words (see what-is-buzzend.md)?

2026 landscape research — findings → what Buzzend does about it

Researched 2026-08-23 (web research across late-2025/2026 sources; key sources inline). This file is the "why" behind the playbooks. Refresh quarterly — algorithms moved a lot in the last year and will again.

The 10 findings that shape this whole system

1 · Short-form ranking is watch-behavior, not follower count. IG ranks Reels on total watch time + likes-per-view + sends (DM shares weighted heaviest); TikTok's bar is ~70% completion; Shorts wants ~65% retention under 30s. (Hootsuite/Dataslayer on the Mosseri signals; Socialync on thresholds.) → We do: 10–35s videos, counter moving by second 2, loopable cuts, send-bait where honest. We track watch %/completion per post, not likes.

2 · Every platform became a search engine. TikTok search, Instagram SEO (captions, spoken words, display name all indexed) and Shorts ranking in Google/YouTube search are primary discovery paths. Hashtag stuffing is dead; 3–5 specific tags + spoken and written keywords is the 2026 consensus. (Later/Toptal on IG SEO; EmbedSocial/Stackmatix on TikTok SEO; Miraflow on Shorts search.) → We do: one search phrase per video, said aloud + on screen + in caption/title. Keyword display names. Shorts get searchable titles.

3 · Sends and saves are the growth currencies. DM-shares (IG) and saves (TikTok) now outweigh likes everywhere. → We do: make save-worthy teaching content (form/setup tips) and send-worthy surprise content (the counter demo). Never ask for likes.

4 · A small team's realistic winning cadence is 3–7 videos/week, cross-posted. Sources conflict on TikTok volume (some say daily×3, Buffer's 11M-post data says 3–5/wk works); everyone agrees quality/completion beats volume, and 0→10k typically takes 3–9 months at consistent cadence. → We do: 5 videos/week (one is always a cheap win: a comment-reply or a repurposed cut), each posted natively to TikTok + Reels + Shorts. No panic before month 3.

5 · The "camera counts your reps" lane is OPEN. Direct rivals exist but none owns the message: Zing AI hides its camera tech behind generic "AI coach" language; Fitnit ("never count reps in your head again") is small; GymCam is utilitarian. Meanwhile the "reps-for-screen-time" boom (Pushscroll ~$30K/mo, Clearspace, PushUp Time…) proved camera-verified reps go viral — framed as anti-doomscrolling, not fitness. → We do: say the plain claim loudly and first ("your phone camera counts your reps"); our wedge vs everyone: no wearable, no hardware, no manual logging — just the phone.

6 · The counter overlay IS the content format. Replo (push-up counter app) grows by users screen-recording their counted sets and posting them; Cal AI scaled to ~$30M/yr on the doctrine "the product must be demonstrable in under 3 seconds on camera." (Starter Story/Stormy on Cal AI.) → We do: every video shows the live counter; we develop screen-recorded "AI counted my set" as our native UGC format and end-of-workout share cards as proof artifacts.

7 · Unpolished beats produced — polished demos read as ads and get scrolled. (TokPortal's tests; Zeely's 92%-want-story stat; Gymshark's lo-fi memes performing at parity with studio work.) → We do: real homes, real clothes, honest reactions, messy-bedroom demos included (pre-empting "does it work in a small dark room?"). Faces beat logos — the team appears on camera; Ladder grew 2,000% by making its people the creators.

8 · The beginner emotional lane is gym anxiety + realistic challenges. "Shy girl workout" (400M+ views), cozy cardio, and the softening of 75Hard into 75Soft/"30 Realistic" all say the same thing: our audience wants judgment-free, doable, at-home. (Healthline, Fortune, Yahoo.) → We do: a whole content pillar on beginner confidence; challenges marketed as realistic and co-op ("finish the week together"), never punishing or physique-focused. Gen Z treats fitness as friendship (Strava's data) — our friends-leaderboard is social connection, not domination.

9 · "Nothing to log" answers the #1 quit reason. Fitness-app churn research: manual entry, complex onboarding and unclear value drive abandonment; only ~3% of installers are active at day 30 industry-wide. Privacy preference is rising (on-device processing). → We do: market "no typing, no logging — press start and move"; if pose data stays on-device, say so plainly ("your camera feed never leaves your phone") — verify with the product team first.

10 · Platform-worthiness verdicts. Facebook: every upload is a Reel pushed to non-followers; Groups reach 20–40% of members — worth 15 min/day. LinkedIn: B2C fitness = not worth team effort; founder 1×/week build-in-public only. X: free-account reach is punitive; claim the handle and park it. → Encoded in the platform playbooks in ../08-social-media/.

Watch-outs the research flagged

  • TikTok US retrained its algorithm in 2026 under the new joint venture — treat old growth lore as expired; our own data is the truth.
  • Duplicate-content throttling is real: one account per platform, clean exports, no multi-account reposting (a tactic some app playbooks use — not for us; spam risk + no hands for it).
  • One research thread says longer (2–3 min) high-retention videos gained reach in 2025. Our default stays 10–35s; we run the occasional "full unedited session, AI counting" 2–3 min experiment to test it.
  • Benchmarks so nobody panics: fitness engagement ~3–4% (IG) / 6–10% (TikTok small accounts), hook rate ≥30–40%, bio-link CTR 2–5%, early videos at 200–1,000 views = normal.

What this research changed vs. the original strategy

The strategy (§20–§23) is confirmed in direction — IG/TikTok/Shorts primary, proof-first content, hooks decide. Research adds: platform SEO as a first-class practice, sends/saves as the metrics to chase, comment-to-DM automation, FB Groups, the counter-overlay UGC format, the gym-anxiety lane, and co-op challenge framing. Research demotes: hashtag volume, likes as a signal, polish. No contradictions with the strategy's guardrails (§7/§40/§47) — all findings fit inside them.

The Buzzend Hook System

A hook is the first 1–3 seconds of a video — what the viewer sees and hears before deciding to keep watching or scroll past. On short-form video, the hook decides ~80% of a video's fate. This file makes hooks a repeatable process instead of a talent.

How to use this system (Person 2, every video)

  1. Pick the video's topic (from 06-content-ideas/idea-backlog.md).
  2. Pick one hook category below that fits the topic.
  3. Use the formula to write 3 hook options.
  4. Say each out loud. Keep the one that sounds like a person, not an ad.
  5. The hook appears three ways at once: spoken in the first seconds, as on-screen text on the first frame, and the first frame also shows the thing (usually: person + counter).
  6. Log the hook + category in the content-idea template so Person 1 can later see which categories win.

The universal rule: whatever the words say, the counter must be visible and moving by second 2. The hook earns attention; the counter keeps it.

The 14 categories

1 · Curiosity

  • What: open a question the brain needs closed.
  • When: product demos for cold audiences (people who've never heard of us).
  • Formula: "Can [unexpected thing] really [do surprising task]?"
  • Buzzend example: "Can my phone really count my push-ups?"
  • Bad: "Check out this cool AI feature." (nothing to be curious about)
  • Better: "I did 30 squats. My phone says 28. One of us is wrong."

2 · Problem

  • What: say the viewer's frustration in their own words.
  • When: reaching people who don't know an app like this exists.
  • Formula: "[The exact annoying moment] — [tiny hint there's a fix]"
  • Buzzend example: "I always lose count at like… 17. So I stopped counting."
  • Bad: "Tired of inefficient workout tracking solutions?" (robot language)
  • Better: "POV: you lost count again and just said 'eh, that was probably 20'."

3 · Mistake

  • What: call out a common error the viewer might be making.
  • When: educational content; earns saves and shares.
  • Formula: "Stop doing [common thing] — do [better thing]"
  • Buzzend example: "Stop counting your reps in your head. You're losing half your focus."
  • Bad: "Top 5 workout mistakes" (generic listicle, seen 1000×)
  • Better: "This squat didn't count — and the app is right. Here's why."

4 · Contrarian

  • What: politely disagree with common advice.
  • When: sparingly — great for comments/debate, risky if smug.
  • Formula: "Everyone says [common advice]. Actually, [our angle]."
  • Buzzend example: "You don't need a gym membership to get consistent. You need a counter."
  • Bad: "Gyms are a scam." (insulting, invites a fight we don't want)
  • Better: "Unpopular opinion: your first workout should be 10 squats. That's it."

5 · Before/After

  • What: show a change between two moments.
  • When: streak stories, progress stories, app-improvement stories.
  • Formula: "[Time ago]: [state A]. Today: [state B]."
  • Buzzend example: "Day 1: 5 wobbly push-ups. Day 14: 20 counted, clean."
  • Bad: dramatic body-transformation claims (we never promise results)
  • Better: "Week 1: I forgot to work out 4 days. Week 3: 21-day streak."

6 · Transformation story

  • What: the journey version of before/after — told as a story.
  • When: weekly series; builds followers who come back.
  • Formula: "Day [N] of [challenge] — here's what happened."
  • Buzzend example: "Day 12 of doing squats until my phone says 50."
  • Bad: "My fitness journey!" (no specifics, no series promise)
  • Better: "Day 12 of 30: today the app caught me cheating a rep. Fair."

7 · Question

  • What: directly ask the audience something they want to answer.
  • When: engagement/community content; feeds comments.
  • Formula: "[Simple either/or or fill-in question about their habits]"
  • Buzzend example: "What number do you always lose count at? Mine's 17."
  • Bad: "What do you think about AI in fitness?" (essay question, nobody answers)
  • Better: "Squats or push-ups — which should the AI count next? 👇"

8 · Challenge

  • What: invite the viewer (or a friend) to attempt something.
  • When: challenge features, weekends, community moments.
  • Formula: "Try [specific, doable feat] — [twist: the app is judging]"
  • Buzzend example: "Try 20 squats with me. The app counts. No cheating possible."
  • Bad: "Join our fitness challenge!" (join what? where? why?)
  • Better: "Bet you can't do 15 push-ups the app actually counts."

9 · Educational

  • What: teach one small useful thing.
  • When: trust-building; the save-magnet category.
  • Formula: "How to [small win] in [tiny time/effort]"
  • Buzzend example: "How to set up your phone so it can see your whole squat."
  • Bad: "Everything you need to know about home workouts" (too big)
  • Better: "Your phone can't count what it can't see — the 10-second camera setup."

10 · Story

  • What: a tiny personal narrative with a beginning and a payoff.
  • When: build-in-public, founder/team content, relatable moments.
  • Formula: "So [normal setup]… then [unexpected turn]."
  • Buzzend example: "I told my brother the app counts reps. He tried to cheat it for 10 minutes."
  • Bad: "Let me tell you about our company's mission." (nobody cares yet)
  • Better: "My mom tested our rep counter. She's 58. It counted all 12."

11 · Social proof

  • What: other people's words/actions vouch for us.
  • When: after real comments/users exist — never fabricate.
  • Formula: "[Real person/comment] said [real reaction] — so [we respond/show]"
  • Buzzend example: "Someone commented 'no way it's real-time'. Okay — watch."
  • Bad: inventing praise or paying for fake reviews (never)
  • Better: screen-record the actual comment, then answer it on camera.

12 · AI / tech curiosity

  • What: the "what does the machine see?" angle.
  • When: the tech-curious slice of our audience; differentiates us.
  • Formula: "What [the AI/camera] sees when you [move]"
  • Buzzend example: "This is what my phone sees during a squat." (show the skeleton overlay)
  • Bad: "Our pose-estimation model uses 33 landmarks…" (tech-speak ban)
  • Better: "The app watches your hips. Don't ask why. (Okay — it's how it counts the rep.)"

13 · Fitness myth

  • What: gently bust a belief that holds beginners back.
  • When: educational slots; pairs with Beginner Confidence pillar.
  • Formula: "'[Myth in quotes]' — here's what actually matters."
  • Buzzend example: "'No pain no gain' is why you quit last time. 10 easy squats a day beats 100 once."
  • Bad: medical claims ("this prevents injury") — banned territory
  • Better: "'I'll start Monday.' It's Thursday. Ten squats. Right now. I'll count — well, my phone will."

14 · Pain point (emotional)

  • What: the deeper feeling under the problem — embarrassment, loneliness, giving up.
  • When: carefully and warmly; never mock the viewer.
  • Formula: "[The feeling nobody says out loud], [permission/relief]"
  • Buzzend example: "Working out alone is boring. That's not a discipline problem — it's a design problem."
  • Bad: shame-based hooks ("still skipping workouts?") — never
  • Better: "Nobody claps when you work out alone at home. Fine — my phone counts out loud now."

Writing rules that apply to every hook

  1. ≤ 10 words on screen. First frame must be readable in one glance.
  2. A real number beats an adjective. "47 squats" > "an amazing workout".
  3. Sound-off test: the on-screen text alone must carry the hook.
  4. No bait-and-switch: the video must deliver what the hook promised.
  5. The viewer's words > our words. Check the hooks-inbox (verbatim comment phrases) before writing from scratch.
  6. Banned words list applies to hooks too (see 00-strategy/what-is-buzzend.md).

How to write a Buzzend video script

A script is ~60–80 spoken words. Writing it takes 20 minutes. Never film without one — "we'll figure it out on camera" produces 45-second rambles.

The structure (same every time)

Seconds Beat What it must do
0–2 HOOK Spoken + on-screen text (≤10 words) + the counter already visible/moving. See ../05-hooks/hook-system.md
2–6 PROOF Wide shot: person + phone + counter climbing. Real reps, real count
6–15 EXPLANATION One sentence: "Buzzend uses your phone camera to count reps while you work out."
15–25 BENEFIT "So you can focus on moving instead of counting." (or the pillar-specific benefit)
last 2–3 CTA End card + spoken: "Try your first AI workout." (challenge content: "Challenge a friend.")

Videos under 15s may drop the BENEFIT beat. Never drop HOOK, PROOF, or CTA.

Rules

  1. Write it as spoken words, read it out loud twice, cut every word that sounds like an ad.
  2. One idea per video. Two ideas = two videos.
  3. The §40 line at the bottom of every script: which phone + camera position was tested, and the verified count.
  4. On-screen text ≤10 words per screen, high contrast, big.

Example script 1 — Pillar 1, curiosity hook (V01, squat demo)

HOOK (0–2s) — Screen text: "My phone just counted my squats." Visual: close-up of the counter ticking 6 → 7. Say: "My phone just counted my squats." PROOF (2–6s) — Wide shot: phone propped on floor, person squatting, counter climbing 8… 9… 10 with the count audible. EXPLANATION (6–15s) — Say: "Buzzend uses your phone camera to count your reps while you work out. No wearable, no equipment — just the phone." BENEFIT (15–25s) — Say: "So you can stop counting in your head and just move." CTA (25–28s) — End card: "Try your first AI workout." + app name. §40 check: iPhone 13, floor-propped, front camera, 12 squats = 12 counted. ✔

CTA (18–21s) — Gentle end card: "Try your first AI workout." §40 check: (fill in device/position/count before filming) ✔

Instagram playbook (Reels + Stories) — PRIMARY

What it is, for us

Reels = short vertical videos shown mostly to people who DON'T follow us (discovery). Stories = 24-hour posts shown to people who DO follow us (relationship). We use Reels to get found and Stories to keep people.

What the algorithm rewards in 2026 (and what that means for us)

Instagram ranks Reels mainly on total watch time (including replays), likes-per-view, and — weighted heaviest — sends (people DMing your Reel to a friend). Practical translation:

  1. Make sendable videos. "The phone counts your reps" is naturally a send-to-a-friend moment. Add send-bait lines where honest: "Send this to your workout buddy who always miscounts."
  2. Make loopable videos. A clean rep loop where the end cuts back into the start gets replays, and replays count as watch time.
  3. Likes matter less than everyone thinks. Don't ask for likes.

Format rules

  • Length: 15–35 seconds. Under 15s only for pure loop clips.

TikTok shows every video to a small test audience regardless of follower count — it's the best platform on earth for a zero-follower account with a visually surprising product. It is also a search engine: our audience literally searches "home workout for beginners" here.

  1. Default to 10–20 second videos — the counter reacting in real time is the easiest 70%-completion clip we can make.

YouTube Shorts playbook — PRIMARY (lowest extra effort)

What it is, for us

Shorts = YouTube's vertical short video feed. Two things make it special:

  1. Shorts rank in YouTube AND Google search like normal videos — a good Short keeps collecting views for months.
  2. We can reuse the exact same videos we make for TikTok/Reels (export clean, no watermarks) — near-zero extra production cost.

What the algorithm rewards in 2026

Retention, with rough thresholds: ~65% watched for sub-30s Shorts (~50% for 30–60s) earns wider distribution. Sweet spot: 15–35 seconds.

The YouTube-only extra work (10 minutes per Short — worth it)

Treat every Short like a tiny search article:

  • Title: keyword-led, under 60 characters, phrased like a search. Good: "Squat Counter App Test: 50 Squats, AI Judged My Form" Bad: "POV: my phone watches me 💀" (fine on TikTok, invisible in search)
  • Description: 3–6 sentences (~150 words) naturally using the words people search: home workout, squat counter, AI workout app, no equipment. Install link in the first 3 lines.
  • Pinned comment on every Short: app name + one-line what-it-is.

The link problem (important)

Links are not tappable inside the Shorts player, and comment links don't work anymore. So on YouTube:

  • Say the app name out loud AND show it on screen in every Short — viewers install by searching the store, and that path is invisible in our link stats (expect YouTube installs to be under-counted).
  • Description link (first 3 lines) + pinned comment carry the rest.

Posting

  • 3–5 Shorts/week, same masters as TikTok/IG (never a watermarked re-upload).
  • Consistent channel identity: handle, avatar, banner with the positioning line.

What success looks like

  • Slower start than TikTok, longer tail. A Short that does nothing for two weeks and then climbs is normal — search takes time.
  • Watch: retention % (aim toward 65% on sub-30s), impressions from search, subscribers gained per Short.

Metrics for beginners — what the numbers mean and which ones matter

Person 1 owns this. Everyone should understand it.

The dictionary (plain language)

Metric What it actually is Where to find it
Impressions How many times our content appeared on screens platform analytics
Reach How many different people saw it platform analytics
Views How many times the video was played per post
Watch time / completion % How much of the video people actually watched per post analytics
Hook rate % of people who stayed past ~3 seconds 3s views ÷ impressions
Likes Taps of approval. Cheap. per post
Comments People talking to us. Valuable. per post
Shares/sends People sending it to friends. VERY valuable. per post
Saves People keeping it for later. VERY valuable. per post
Followers People subscribed to us profile
Profile visits People interested enough to check who we are account analytics
Link clicks Taps on our bio/Story/description links link-in-bio tool
Store visits People who reached our App Store / Play page App Store Connect / Play Console
Installs Downloads store consoles
Conversion % moving from one step to the next (e.g. installs ÷ store visits) computed

The hierarchy — which numbers actually matter

Think of it as a funnel. Each level matters only because it feeds the next:

views → watch %/completion → shares+saves → profile visits
→ link clicks → store visits → INSTALLS → (product) first workouts

The winner rule: a post is good if it moves the BOTTOM of the funnel (store visits → installs), not the top. A 100k-view video that drives 3 store visits lost to a 5k-view video that drives 60.

Vanity warning: likes and follower count are the least meaningful numbers on this page. We never celebrate or panic over them.

What "normal" looks like (so nobody quits in week 2)

  • Early videos: 200–1,000 views is normal. The occasional outlier carries the account.
  • Fitness engagement rate: ~3–4% on IG, 6–10% on small TikToks = healthy.
  • Hook rate: ≥30% (IG) / ≥40% (TikTok) = good hook.
  • Completion: climb toward 70% on sub-20s TikToks, 65% on sub-30s Shorts.
  • Bio-link clicks: 2–5% of profile visitors. Installs will lag views badly at first. That's normal, not failure.
  • Reaching 10k followers organically typically takes 3–9 months of consistent posting. Month 1 is for finding what works.

The weekly routine (P1, Fridays, ~1 h)

  1. Fill the weekly analytics report (template #11) from the daily metrics-sheet rows.
  2. Rank the week's posts by the winner rule.
  3. Verdict every post: WON / LOST / RETEST (recorded on its calendar row).
  4. Bring 3 decisions to Monday's meeting: repeat / change / stop.

The learning loop (never post-and-forget)

POST → MEASURE (24h + 7d) → WHAT WORKED? → WHAT FAILED?
→ CREATE VARIATION (change ONE thing) → RETEST → SCALE WINNERS

Decision rules:

  • CONTINUE — beat our median on funnel metrics → make the next variation now (new exercise/setting/wording — one change).
  • IMPROVE — great hook rate but weak finish → keep the hook, fix the payoff/CTA; or great watch % but no link taps → fix the CTA.
  • REPURPOSE — worked on one platform, flat on another → re-cut with platform-native title/caption before giving up on it.
  • STOP — two attempts of the same angle both below median → kill the angle, write one line about why in the weekly report.
  • TEST AGAIN — numbers contradict each other or sample too small (<500 views) → repost variation at a better time before judging.

Every experiment gets an experiment-tracker entry (template #13): one variable, a hypothesis, a verdict. That discipline is what makes three beginners smarter every single week.
